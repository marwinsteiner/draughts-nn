\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:concl}

We built an engine for international draughts that combines a conventional
alpha-beta search with a small neural network trained to imitate Scan, and
deployed it on lidraughts.org as valhalla. In three days of blitz it played 127
games. It did not lose to a human: against 16 players rated up to 2293 it won 71
games and drew 5. It needed only a handful of games to be rated above almost every
player on the site, and it now stands above 99.98\% of the month's blitz players,
with one human on the leaderboard rated higher. Its strength against humans comes
from consistency rather than insight: the humans made the mistakes that decided
the games, mostly in the middlegame, and the engine made almost none. Against
other programs nearly every game was drawn, and against Scan the engine is still
about 93 Elo weaker. Learning from individual games added little measurable
strength; retraining on more varied data added more.

The most direct next step is to train on the engine's own positions. Positions
from Scan playing itself do not cover the positions valhalla reaches in its own
games, and we are generating such data now: games played by the engine, against
itself and against Scan, with every position scored by Scan. A second step is a
faster search. In our measurements the engine searches about a third as many
positions per second as Scan on the same machine, so a faster move generator and
evaluation would let the same network look further ahead. Since most draws ended
with four or fewer pieces on the board, endgame databases, which store the exact
result of every position with few pieces, would tell the engine which advantages
can be won and which cannot.

Two changes concern how the engine is improved and how it plays for rating. A
sequential test, which keeps playing until the difference between two networks
is clear, would stop networks that are no better from replacing the current one
more reliably than a fixed 200-game match with a 49\% threshold. And because a
draw against a much lower-rated human costs many times what a win earns, the
engine could prefer positions that keep winning chances alive over safe but
drawish ones when its opponent is much weaker.

Two further ideas go beyond the present design. The engine's strength is
currently bounded by Scan, whose judgement it imitates; self-play reinforcement
learning, as in AlphaZero \parencite{silver2018}, could take it beyond its
teacher, at a much higher cost in computation. Finally, blitz favours the engine.
Games at slower time controls, and against the site's highest-rated players,
would show how much of its advantage over humans is due to time pressure.
