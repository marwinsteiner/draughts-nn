\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

The engine plays on lidraughts.org under the name valhalla. This section reports
every blitz game it played between 24 October 2026, 10:36 UTC (its first game),
and 26 October 2026, 21:05 UTC: 127 games in total. We first list the games
(\cref{sec:res-games}), then show how its rating developed and where that rating
places it among the site's players (\cref{sec:res-rating}), how it fared
against humans (\cref{sec:res-humans}) and against Scan (\cref{sec:res-scan}),
and finally how its games were won, drawn and lost (\cref{sec:res-how}).

\subsection{Games played}\label{sec:res-games}

Lidraughts keeps a separate rating for each speed of play. We study blitz, which
on lidraughts means games with an estimated duration of roughly 3 to 8 minutes
per player (3 minutes plus 2 seconds per move, for example). Of the 127 blitz
games, 76 were against humans and 51 against three other bots (Adder,
Virtual-Draughts and Viral-Bot), the only bots on the site that accept games of
international draughts. The most common time controls were 3 minutes with no
increment (63 games) and 3 minutes plus 2 seconds per move (52 games). One game
was unrated.

\subsection{Rating}\label{sec:res-rating}

Lidraughts rates players with the Glicko-2 system \parencite{glickman2001}. Every
new account starts at 1500 with a large uncertainty, and while the uncertainty is
large each game moves the rating a lot; the site marks such a rating with a
question mark (``provisional''). \Cref{fig:rating} shows valhalla's blitz rating
after each rated blitz game, and \cref{fig:distribution} shows where it sits among
the 6{,}308 players who played rated blitz on lidraughts in the past month
\parencite{lidraughts_stats}.

The engine needed very few games to rise above almost every player on the site.
After one game its rating (1830) was above 85\% of the monthly players; after 5
games it was above 99\% of them, and after 9 games above 99.9\%. All of these
games were against bots: its first game against a human was its 13th, by which
time it was already rated 2304. Part of this speed is due to Glicko-2 itself,
which moves a new player's rating quickly; but the rating did not fall back
once the system had more confidence in it. The question mark disappeared after
24 games, at 2407, and over the following 102 games the rating stayed between
2395 and 2470.

After its last game the engine was rated 2459, higher than 99.98\% of the
monthly blitz players. On the blitz leaderboard, which lists human players only,
one player is rated higher (2506); the next is rated 2400.

\subsection{Against humans}\label{sec:res-humans}

No human player has beaten the engine. In 76 blitz games against 16 different
players it won 71 and drew 5 (\cref{tab:record}). Its opponents were rated
between 1950 and 2293 (median 2026) at the time of the game; the strongest of
them, rated 2291--2293, played it three times, drawing twice and losing once.
Of the 71 wins, 47 ended when the opponent resigned, 14 when the opponent ran out
of time, and 10 when the opponent had no legal move left (\cref{tab:endings}).
A won game against a human lasted a median of 90 plies, that is 45 moves by
each player.

\subsection{Against Scan}\label{sec:res-scan}

To measure the engine against a fixed and much stronger opponent, we matched
networks against Scan on the same 400 games (\cref{sec:arena,sec:learning}).
The networks champ\_000 to champ\_003 played the first 20 games, up to
25 October, 09:00 UTC; champ\_004 played from then on, except while a lesson had replaced it with
champ\_005 or, from 26 October 18:11 UTC, with champ\_006, which has not yet been
measured against Scan. \Cref{tab:scan} lists the results. The best network,
champ\_004, scored 36.9\% against Scan, about 93 Elo below it. On this measure the engine is
still clearly weaker than the program that taught it. Retraining from scratch on
more and deeper data (v2) gained 20 Elo over the sparring fine-tunes; adding yet
more of the same kind of data (v3) did not help.

\subsection{How games were won, drawn and lost}\label{sec:res-how}

To see what happened inside the games, we let Scan judge every position of every
blitz game, searching 1{,}000{,}000 nodes per position, and express its
evaluation in men from valhalla's point of view: $+1$ means valhalla is a man
ahead, or has an advantage worth as much. Because draughts pieces only ever leave
the board, the number of pieces on the board is a natural clock for the stage of
the game. We call a position part of the opening while more than 30 of the 40
pieces remain, part of the middlegame with 16 to 30 pieces, and part of the
endgame with 15 or fewer. We call a won or lost game decided from the
first position after which the winner stayed at least one man ahead, by Scan's
evaluation, until the end of the game (after smoothing the evaluation with a
running median over five plies, so that a single noisy score does not move this
point). \Cref{fig:trajectories} shows the evaluation over each game,
\cref{fig:decided} the stage at which each game was decided or ended, and
\cref{fig:errors} how much evaluation each side gave away per move.

The wins against humans follow one pattern. Through the
opening both sides play almost equally well, giving away 0.018 (valhalla) and
0.028 (humans) men per move on average, and the evaluation stays level. In the
middlegame the humans start to make mistakes: they give away 0.096 men per move
against valhalla's 0.028, and 1.8\% of their moves lose a man or more at once. In
the endgame this grows to 0.172 men per move and 4.2\% of moves. In 3{,}137
moves in open positions against humans, valhalla did not make a single move that
lost a man. Of the 71 wins, 65 were decided on the board, a median of 71 plies
into the game with 18 pieces left (half of them between 16 and 24 pieces): 46 in
the middlegame, 16 in the endgame and 3 in the opening. In 46 of these 65 games
the move that decided the game was the human's own; in the other 19 it was a move
by valhalla that turned a smaller advantage into one of at least a man. The 6
wins not decided on the board are 4 games in which the human ran out of time in
a level position and 2 in which the human resigned when about 0.9 men behind.

Against the bots almost every game was drawn (44 of 51). Here
neither side gave much away: both valhalla and its bot opponents lost 0.012 to
0.031 men per move at every stage, and hardly ever a whole man
(\cref{fig:errors}). In 39 of the 44 draws valhalla was never a man ahead by
Scan's judgement. These games were long (a median of 167 plies) and all ended in
the endgame, with 2 to 8 pieces left (median 4), by the 5-move rule (15), the
16-move rule (12), threefold repetition (11) or the 25-move rule (6). In 4 draws
valhalla reached an advantage of at least 2 men and did not convert it. To check
whether valhalla's endgame play was to blame, we took 12 positions from three
draws against Virtual-Draughts in which valhalla's own search judged it at least
a man ahead, and let Scan and valhalla play each of them twice with each colour
(48 games at 5 seconds plus 0.05 seconds per move). All 48 games were drawn, so
these advantages were most likely not enough to win. The 5 draws against humans
also ended in endgames of 3 or 4 pieces; in none of them was valhalla more than
0.9 men ahead.

All 5 losses were against bots. Three of them were losses on time. All three
were played on 25 October between 19:10 and 20:51 UTC against Viral-Bot, in
games without an increment, and in all three Scan judged the position at the end
to be within half a man of level. They were caused by a fault in the engine's time
management that we fixed at about 21:00 UTC that day; none of the 73 games
played since has been lost on time. The two losses on the board, one against
Adder (24 October) and one against Viral-Bot (26 October), look alike: no single
move lost a man, but valhalla's position slowly worsened through the middlegame
until Scan judged it a man behind (from 20 and 26 pieces on the board
respectively), and the game ended many moves later with valhalla left without a
legal move. The second of these was played by champ\_006, a network that the
lesson procedure of \cref{sec:learning} had put in place after it scored 49.5\%
in its 200-game match against champ\_004; of its 27 games so far it has won 8,
drawn 18 and lost this one.
