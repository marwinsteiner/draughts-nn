Programs that play board games have been built for more than seventy years, and
draughts has been part of that story from the start. This section follows four
threads that lead to our engine: the early idea that a machine could play a game
of skill at all (\cref{sec:lit-origins}); the long line of programs for checkers,
the $8\times8$ form of draughts (\cref{sec:lit-checkers}); the move from
hand-written evaluation rules to evaluation functions that are learned, in
checkers and in chess, backgammon, shogi and Go (\cref{sec:lit-learning}); and
the engines built for international draughts, the $10\times10$ game we play
(\cref{sec:lit-international}).

\subsection{Early ideas}\label{sec:lit-origins}

The idea that a machine could play a game of skill is older than the electronic
computer. \textcite{babbage1864} describes how he worked out that a machine could
play noughts and crosses, and argues that a machine could in principle play any
game of pure skill, chess included, by examining the moves available to it.

The plan that every later engine follows was written down by
\textcite{shannon1950}. A program should look ahead through the possible moves
of both players, building a tree of positions, and judge the positions at the end
of each line with an evaluation function, a formula that turns a position into a
number saying how good it is for one side. The program then assumes that each
player picks the move that is best for them, which is the minimax rule, and plays
the move at the root that leads to the best result under that assumption. Shannon
also pointed out that the full tree of chess is far too large to search, so a
program must either stop at a fixed depth or look only at promising moves.
\textcite{turing1953} described a chess procedure of the same kind, simple enough
to be carried out by hand. The most important technique for making such searches
faster is alpha-beta pruning, which skips branches that cannot change the result;
\textcite{knuth1975} gave its first full analysis, and our search
(\cref{sec:search}) is built on it.

\subsection{Checkers}\label{sec:lit-checkers}

The first program to play a complete game of draughts was written by Christopher
Strachey for the Ferranti Mark 1 computer in Manchester and was running by the
summer of 1952. He presented it at the Association for Computing Machinery
meeting in Toronto that year \parencite{strachey1952}. It looked a few moves
ahead and chose the move that seemed best after that limited look-ahead.

Arthur Samuel at IBM went one step further and wrote the first checkers program
that learned to play better \parencite{samuel1959}. Its evaluation function was a
weighted sum of board features chosen by Samuel, such as the material balance
and the control of the centre. The program adjusted the weights while playing
against itself: it compared the value its evaluation gave a position with the
value a deeper search later found for the same position, and moved the weights so
that the two agreed better. This idea of training a quick judgement to match the
result of a deeper look is the same idea we use when we train our network on
Scan's search scores (\cref{sec:data}). Samuel's program also stored positions it
had already evaluated so it did not have to repeat the work, and his paper is one
of the earliest uses of the term machine learning. In 1962 the program won a
widely reported game against Robert Nealey, but later analysis showed that Nealey
played weak moves in that game and that the program was far from master
strength \parencite{schaeffer1997}. Samuel's later version replaced the weighted
sum with signature tables, which let features combine in non-linear ways, and
used alpha-beta pruning, but it still could not beat checkers masters
\parencite{samuel1967}.

The program that finally reached the top was Chinook, built by Jonathan
Schaeffer's group at the University of Alberta
\parencite{schaeffer1992,schaeffer1996}. Chinook combined a deep alpha-beta
search, an evaluation function tuned by hand with expert knowledge, a book of
opening moves, and endgame databases that store the exact result of every
position with few enough pieces left on the board. In 1992 it played a match for
the world title against Marion Tinsley, who won four games, lost two and drew 33.
This was the first time a human world champion had defended a title against a
computer. In the 1994 rematch Tinsley withdrew because of illness after six drawn
games, and Chinook became the first program to hold a human world championship
title \parencite{schaeffer1996}. The work culminated in the proof that checkers
is a draw: with perfect play by both sides, neither can win
\parencite{schaeffer2007}. The proof combined endgame databases for all positions
with ten or fewer pieces with a search from the starting position, and computers
had been working on it almost continuously since 1989.

\subsection{Learned evaluation functions}\label{sec:lit-learning}

Chinook's strength came from search and from human knowledge written into its
evaluation function. A parallel line of work asked whether a program could learn
a good evaluation function with little or no human knowledge.

In backgammon, \textcite{tesauro1995} trained a neural network by letting it play
against itself and nudging its evaluation of each position towards its evaluation
of the next position, a method called temporal difference learning. The program,
TD-Gammon, reached the level of the best human players.

In checkers, \textcite{chellapilla1999} trained neural networks by evolution
rather than by gradient descent. Each network took the board as 32 numbers, one
per playable square, passed them through two hidden layers of 40 and 10 units, and
produced one number that served as the evaluation inside a minimax search. The
value of a king relative to a man was evolved as well. A population of networks
played games against each other, the networks that scored best survived, and
their copies were changed by small random mutations to form the next generation.
No human games and no expert features were used, only the win, loss or draw of
each game. Later versions added a first layer that looked at every square
sub-region of the board separately, and after about 840 generations the best
network played at expert level against people on an internet gaming site
\parencite{chellapilla2001,fogel2002}. This work was the starting point of our
project: our first prototype, kept in the repository, reimplemented their
32--40--10--1 network, the evolved king value and the tournament selection for
$8\times8$ checkers.

In chess, the strongest programs of the 1990s took the opposite route to
learning. Deep Blue beat the world champion Garry Kasparov in a match in 1997,
using special-purpose hardware to search hundreds of millions of positions per
second with a large evaluation function tuned by hand
\parencite{campbell2002}.

The game of Go resisted this approach, because its positions are hard to judge
with hand-written rules. AlphaGo combined deep neural networks with Monte Carlo
tree search: a policy network suggested promising moves, a value network judged
positions, and both were trained first on human games and then by self-play
\parencite{silver2016}. It was the first program to beat a professional Go
player in an even game. AlphaGo Zero removed the human games and learned from
self-play alone \parencite{silver2017}, and AlphaZero applied the same method to
chess, shogi and Go and beat the strongest existing program in each
\parencite{silver2018}.

These systems need large networks and much computing power to evaluate each
position. A different design, built for programs that search many positions per
second on an ordinary processor, came from computer shogi. \textcite{nasu2018}
proposed an efficiently updatable neural network, now usually called NNUE, whose
first layer does not have to be recomputed after every move. Most of a position
is unchanged by a move, so the first layer is updated by adding and subtracting
the contributions of the few pieces that moved. This keeps a neural evaluation
cheap enough to use inside a fast alpha-beta search. Our network follows this
design (\cref{sec:network}).

A related idea is to train a small model to reproduce the outputs of a larger or
stronger one, known as knowledge distillation \parencite{hinton2015}. We use the
same principle with a strong draughts engine as the teacher: our network learns
to predict the search scores of Scan (\cref{sec:training}).

\subsection{International draughts}\label{sec:lit-international}

International draughts is played on a $10\times10$ board with 20 pieces a side.
Kings move any distance along a diagonal, and a player must make the capture that
takes the most pieces. Positions therefore have more moves and longer capture
sequences than in checkers, and the game has not been solved.

Programs for the $10\times10$ game appeared later than for checkers. Draughts
was part of the first Computer Olympiad in London in 1989, where the papers
included a draughts program that learned from its own play
\parencite{smeets1989} and a discussion of draughts programming by Derek
Oldbury, a former world checkers champion \parencite{oldbury1989}, and the
$10\times10$ game has been played at the Olympiad since then. Truus, by Stef Keetman, won silver at the first Olympiad and gold at the
third, in Maastricht in 1991 \parencite{cpw_keetman}, and other strong programs
of the following decades include Buggy, Dam, Damage, Dragon and the
international version of Kingsrow \parencite{cpw_draughts}. In 2012 the former
world champion Alexander Schwarzman played a six-game match against the program
Maximus, winning one game and drawing the other five \parencite{vanhorssen2012}.

The strongest freely available engine today is Scan by Fabien Letouzey, which won
the draughts tournament of the 18th Computer Olympiad in Leiden in 2015
\parencite{cpw_draughts}. Scan is an alpha-beta engine with a fast evaluation
function, an opening book and endgame bitbases, and it is released as open
source together with the Hub protocol through which programs and graphical
interfaces talk to draughts engines \parencite{letouzey_scan}. The online platform
lidraughts.org, where our engine plays, uses Scan to analyse games
\parencite{lidraughts}.

Our engine sits where these threads meet. Like Chinook and Scan, it relies on a
fast alpha-beta search. Like Samuel's program and the work of Chellapilla and
Fogel, it learns its evaluation function instead of having it written by hand.
Like NNUE, it uses a small network whose first layer is updated incrementally, so
that the evaluation stays cheap enough for deep search. Unlike the systems above,
its training targets come from a stronger engine acting as a teacher rather than
from human games or from self-play alone, and it keeps learning after it is
deployed, from the games against people on a public server that it loses, or
draws against much weaker players.
